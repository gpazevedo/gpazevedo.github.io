The Sink Was a Lie

What breaks when the destination stops being a stand-in.

TL;DR

The problem. You have read the records at high throughput. Now save them — durably and idempotently, into a real database, and keep a second system in sync without losing or duplicating rows when the process dies mid-flush.

What you get. Five stages on the write side: a real Cassandra sink and what at-least-once actually costs; why no ordering makes a dual write safe; replacing that dual write with CDC off the write-ahead log; when an outbox earns its place and when it does not; and what object-backed storage does to a broker you have to replace.

Why it is worth your time. Every claim is reconciled against a tally the client does not produce — a SELECT COUNT(*), a topic's end offsets, a count(*) in Postgres — including the claims that came out negative. One of the five stages concludes you should not adopt the thing it just built.

The code. gpazevedo/api-consumer — the sink work is client/sinks.py and client/projector.py, one merge commit per stage.

Part 2 of 2 — Part 1 is about reading the data. This half is about landing it.

The first post ended with a list of things the client could not do. I wrote that list on purpose, because the failures in this kind of code return exit code 0, and the only defence is writing down what you did not check.

The second item on it was this:

The sink is a stand-in. Its write awaits nothing real, which is why the worker pool shows no throughput gain here.

That one line is the reason for everything below. Sink.written += len(rows) cannot fail, cannot be slow, and cannot be written to twice with the same row and notice. Which means three claims made earlier in the build were not conclusions. They were assumptions wearing a test.

The claimMade atWhy it was unfalsifiable
At-least-once is safe, because the write is idempotent on id Stage 4 You cannot make a counter non-idempotent.
Batching beats one write per row Stage 7 There was no round trip to amortise.
The worker pool overlaps the write tail Stage 8 Measured 4%, and called it noise.

So I made the destination real, and then followed whatever broke. Five more stages.

The method, unchanged

The numbers below are all from live runs against an adversarial mock server, reconciled against tallies the client does not produce — a SELECT COUNT(*), a topic's end offsets, a count(*) in Postgres. Every claim still has a counterpart it cannot fake.

Part I — Three claims, finally falsifiable

The first real destination is Cassandra, and picking it settles an argument rather than starting one. A Cassandra INSERT against a primary key is a blind write: no read, no compare, no lightweight transaction. That is "idempotent on id, last-write-wins". The bet the client made stages ago is now the storage engine's documented behaviour instead of a hopeful comment.

Two things surprised me.

⊗
A multi-partition BATCH is not a bulk write

It is a coordinator-side atomicity tool, and using it for throughput makes throughput worse: 500 buffered rows across 500 partitions means one coordinator fans out to every replica, having thrown away the token-aware routing that would have sent each row straight to a node that owns it.

⇄
The driver is threaded and this client is not

execute_async returns a ResponseFuture, and the reply arrives on the driver's own IO thread. add_callbacks plus loop.call_soon_threadsafe onto a loop.create_future() is ten lines — and those ten lines are what two packaged async Cassandra clients are asking to be adopted over.

So 500 rows are 500 concurrent single-row upserts under a semaphore, and a test makes constructing a BatchStatement raise. The batching still pays — one flush, one checkpoint, one syscall for the rejects — just not in the CQL. And neither packaged client is worth a C++ dependency or a wrapper around ten lines.

Then the payoff. Twenty thousand records, SIGKILL two and a half seconds in:

5,842 rows were durable ahead of the checkpoint that covered them — which is the safe direction for that inequality to point, and exactly why the resumed run re-fetched them. The server served seven thousand records more than exist. The row count did not move.

That is the entire argument for at-least-once delivery, and until the sink was real there was no way to make it.

The third claim did not survive, and that is a result too

The wall-clock differences sit inside the run-to-run spread. The CPU number does not, and it is stable everywhere. The client is CPU-bound on one thread, so there is no idle time for a pool to overlap into: fifty thousand rows means fifty thousand tasks, semaphore acquisitions, futures, query plans and protocol CRCs, none of which are on the wire.

The old "4% is noise" verdict survives contact with a real database. But it survives with a reason attached instead of a shrug, and the reason changes what to do next. More coroutines cannot help. More processes can.
Reuse this

A blind upsert on a primary key is what makes at-least-once safe — no read, no compare, no lightweight transaction. Skip the multi-partition BATCH: prepared per-row statements keep token-aware routing, and the batching pays in your own buffer instead.

Part II — Two destinations, and no transaction between them

The records are wanted in two places: a store to serve reads from, and a log other services can fan out from without asking this client for anything. So the flush grows a step.

first
Rejects to disk
Unchanged since stage 4. Append-only, never discarded.
then
Rows to Cassandra
The buffered batch, as concurrent single-row upserts.
then
Events to the topic
The new step — and the one with nothing holding it to the step above.
✗ a crash lands here and no ordering prevents it
last
The cursor advances
Still last. Still the only ordering the client fully controls.

And the extra step buys nothing. There is no transaction across Cassandra and Redpanda. A crash between the two writes is not prevented by deciding which one goes first. The ordering only decides which side loses.

I did not want to argue that in prose, so I measured it in both directions.

The sign flips with the order. The magnitude never reaches zero.

Same walk, same kill, twenty thousand records — six runs, three per direction:

The sign flips with the order. The magnitude never reaches zero. A clean run lands 20,000 and 20,000, which is what makes the crashed numbers mean something instead of being noise about a broken build.

Retrying does not close it either — whichever write went first has already committed, so a retry that also fails leaves the same inconsistency one flush later.

A second destination found a bug the first one structurally could not

The buffer emptied itself before the write. With a sink that cannot fail, nothing ever noticed. With a destination that can refuse: the rows are gone from memory, the exception skips the checkpoint's completion — and then the unconditional exit flush finds an empty buffer, succeeds, and advances the cursor over records nothing ever wrote. A lost batch and a checkpoint certifying it.

The fix is one line moved. Empty the buffer only after the write returns.

A buffer that empties before the write is a checkpoint that lies.

Reuse this

There is no transaction across two systems, and no retry closes the gap. Ordering only picks which side loses. And whatever you write to, empty the buffer only after the write returns — otherwise the exit flush certifies a batch nobody stored.

Part III — One write, or none

The dual write cannot be made reliable by ordering, so the next stage stops trying. The walk's only destination becomes Postgres: one transaction per flush, INSERT ... ON CONFLICT (id) DO UPDATE. Debezium tails the write-ahead log and publishes row images to the topic. A small projector consumes that topic and upserts into Cassandra — through the same sink object, unchanged, which is the moment the earlier stage pays for itself. Neither end of that sink knows the rows now arrive from a replication slot instead of an HTTP page.

The dual write is not made reliable. It is made impossible.

There is one thing to write. The log is derived from it by the database, out of a WAL the database was writing anyway, so there is no second write for a crash to land between. The fix is structural rather than probabilistic, and the numbers show it — the same kill that produced the table above:

Exact, not small. Against +801 and −863 on the same walk one stage earlier.

Now the honest part

CDC does not remove the duplicate; it moves it. Two hundred thousand records, Debezium hard-killed three seconds into streaming and restarted:

200,000
rows in Postgres
212,288
events on the topic
200,000

Twelve thousand duplicate events, republished because Debezium's offsets file was behind the WAL it had already sent. The projector does nothing clever about them and does not need to, because the write at the far end is still idempotent on id. The size of that duplicate is an offset-flush interval — a number in a properties file, not a guarantee.

The same invariant, a third mechanism

The projector holds the line every durable step in this client holds: a consumer group offset is a checkpoint by another name, so it commits after the write, never before. Committing first is stage 7's lying checkpoint at a third destination.

There is a cost, and it is worth stating. End to end — one COMMIT to a visible row in Cassandra — is roughly a second. Nothing in this build makes a reader wait for it, so a query answered from the read store can be behind the row committed in Postgres. That is what you buy with the structural fix.

Reuse this

Derive the second system from the first instead of writing to both: one transaction, and CDC off the write-ahead log the database was writing anyway. A consumer group offset is a checkpoint by another name — commit it after the write, never before.

Part IV — What an outbox actually buys

Nothing broke to get here. That is the point of this part.

If Debezium is tailing the WAL, the WAL already contains the record writes. An outbox table adds a second write of the same data inside the same transaction. So what does it buy? I built it to find out, and the answer was allowed to be "nothing".

It buys exactly two things.

✓
The event is authored, not derived

Rename a column — payload to body — and push one row through each path. The table-image consumer reading after.payload raises KeyError. The outbox consumer is unchanged, because its payload was never a column layout; it is a document the producer wrote, carrying its own version field.

✓
Not every event is a row

This codebase already had the example and I had not noticed. A rejected record never becomes a row — it goes to rejects.jsonl and stops there — so no CDC configuration on the records table can ever emit record.rejected.

A table-image consumer is reading a schema, so a DBA's migration is its outage. One walk, twenty thousand records at a 5% malformed rate:

Counted inValue
Rows in records19,001
Events of type record.ingested19,001
Events of type record.rejected999
Lines in rejects.jsonl999

Nine hundred and ninety-nine events that no amount of CDC on the row table could have produced.

And then the verdict, which the ledger allows to be negative:

If your events are row images, do not adopt the outbox. It is a second write of the same bytes, an extra table to prune, and one more thing between a commit and a consumer. CDC on the tables was already giving you that.

Adopt it when the event is not the row — when its shape is a contract you version, or when there are events with no row behind them at all. That is the same bar I apply to libraries, pointed at a pattern instead of a package.
Reuse this

Adopt the outbox only when the event is not the row — a payload you version rather than a schema a migration changes under you, or events with no row behind them at all. If your events are row images, CDC on the tables already gave you that.

Part V — The partition that cannot move

One thing left that none of the stages above can see: a broker has to be replaced. A node dies, an instance is reclaimed, a cluster is rebalanced. On Kafka and on Redpanda, moving a partition means copying what is in it, and that takes as long as the bytes take.

AutoMQ's pitch is that it does not, because the bytes are in an object store already and only the metadata moves. That claim is on its own website and I could not find it quantified against anything. So I measured it.

The client diff is one function. Both speak the Kafka protocol, so the producer, the projector and Debezium's sink all change by one address. Fifteen stages ago I wrote down a rule that looked like pedantry — the cursor is opaque, never decoded, only handed back — and said it would be collected on at the end. This is the same discipline collecting a second time: nothing above the wire knew which broker it was talking to, so nothing above the wire had to be edited.

The claim under test is the slope, not the seconds:

Sixteen times the data is sixteen times the wait — on one of them. The other has no slope to fit.

Redpanda is a straight line — about 1.1 seconds of fixed cost plus 10 ms per megabyte, so sixteen times the data is sixteen times the wait. AutoMQ has no slope to fit. The 2 GB move was faster than the 512 MB one, which is what "no relationship" looks like when you plot three points.

Eighty times slower, because every durable write goes to the object store instead of a local disk. That is one machine's number, and a tuned AutoMQ against real S3 is a different measurement — but it is the opposite of the caveat I expected to be writing, so it goes in as measured.

The two caveats I did expect, printed rather than buried: a loopback copy between containers on one box flatters Redpanda against a real cross-AZ network, and a local object store flatters AutoMQ's read latency. Which is exactly why the finding is the shape of the curve.

A single machine can honestly distinguish flat from linear. It cannot honestly quote seconds.

Reuse this

Keep every address behind one accessor and nothing above the wire needs to know which cluster it is talking to. When you compare two systems, measure the shape of the curve, not the seconds — one machine can tell flat from linear honestly, and cannot quote absolute numbers honestly.

Part VI — The benchmark lied four times before it told the truth

This is the part I would keep if I had to throw the rest away.

Every one of these produced a plausible number instead of an error:

What was wrongWhat it looked like
Dialled the port of a broker I had deleted A producer retrying for fifteen minutes — indistinguishable from a slow fill
Polled for "successfully completed"; the tool prints "is completed" Polling forever against a move that had already finished
Stopped timing when the controller's in-flight list emptied A 128 MB partition timed at 40 ms, still sitting on the broker it started on
Batched sixteen records into a request over the broker's size limit "35 MB/s", into a partition holding zero records

Look at the direction of those errors. Every single one biases the result towards the thing I was hoping to find. A move that did not happen is the fastest move there is. A partition that is empty moves instantly. I was not being dishonest; I was being ordinary, and ordinary is enough.

So the rig grew two assertions that have nothing to do with brokers.

✓
move.py asserts the landing

The partition actually arrived on the broker it was sent to. Without it, a move that never happened is timed as the fastest move in the dataset.

✓
fill.py asks the broker

How many records it actually holds, rather than trusting its own loop counter — the same rule the client has followed since the beginning, pointed at the benchmark instead of the client.

That second check immediately caught a fifth one: 512 records sent, 573 in the partition, because a leader re-election in the first seconds after startup made the producer retry and the retries were not idempotent. A partition 12% larger than the label it was about to be given.

There was a sixth, and it is the most instructive. The first version of the rig shelled out to each cluster's own CLI — rpk on one side, kafka-reassign-partitions.sh on the other. The JVM that second one starts costs about three seconds per poll.

8 ms
the effect being measured
3,000 ms

2

I was about to measure an eight-millisecond effect with a three-second ruler, using two different rulers on the two things being compared. Both clusters speak the Kafka protocol, so the fix was to write one program that talks to both and put no docker exec anywhere in the rig.

A rig whose noise floor exceeds its effect can only confirm what it was built to confirm.

Reuse this

Assert that the thing you are measuring actually happened — that the partition landed, that the records are really in it. Ask the system, never your own loop counter. Every bug in this rig produced a plausible number, and every one biased the answer toward what it was built to find.

What the arc adds up to

Idea 1 The checkpoint must never advance past durability now four mechanisms deep
rejects before cursor contiguous prefix the buffer empties after the write offsets commit after the write
Idea 2 Idempotence is what makes at-least-once affordable a crash, a CDC restart, a broker retry
7,000 replayed records 12,288 duplicate events no dedup logic anywhere
Idea 3 Say what you cannot do the list got longer, not shorter
CDC is at-least-once too the read store lags one machine's numbers

The checkpoint must never advance past durability. It showed up again in two new places here: a buffer that empties before its write, and a consumer group offset committed before the projector's write. Same invariant, third and fourth mechanism, and every violation of it produces a success message.

Idempotence is what makes at-least-once affordable. It absorbed a crash replay, then twelve thousand duplicate CDC events, then a broker's retry. Not one of those needed dedup logic, and each of them would have needed exactly-once otherwise — which is not purchasable at any of these boundaries.

Say what you cannot do. The list got longer, not shorter:

What it still cannot doWhy it is written down instead of fixed
Deliver each event exactly once CDC removes the dual write, not the duplicate. A Debezium restart republished 12,288 events, absorbed only because the far write is idempotent on id.
Serve a read that is current The read store lags by roughly a second, and nothing here makes a reader wait for it.
Prune the outbox Stage 15 writes it and never deletes from it. A real deployment removes rows once the WAL has carried them.
Tell you how many seconds a partition move takes Two containers on one box copy bytes over loopback. Flat versus linear is honest at this scale; absolute numbers are not.
Survive losing the object store Stage 16 moves the durability of every partition into it — a replication factor you no longer manage in exchange for a dependency you now do. This build never tests the failure it introduces.

The item I got to delete was the one this whole arc was about. The sink is not a stand-in any more, and every claim that depended on it is now either measured or withdrawn.
