Granular: what a five-day spike taught us about concept-level curriculum mapping

In five days, a spec-driven spike built Granular: a system that reads a university's published CS catalogue, maps each course to concepts in the CS2023 curriculum standard, infers which concepts depend on which, and answers “what should I learn next?” in plain English.

TL;DR

The spike showed that a concept-level curriculum map can be built and honestly measured.

It did not yet show that the map works at the grain of weekly learning modules, which is the next question.

Why a spike

Curriculum systems treat a course as one large, fixed block. The catalogue says CS 374 requires CS 225, but not which ideas from CS 225 it actually builds on. Our initiative wants to move from that “macro” view to an “atomic” one: if a student struggles in a week's module, the system should be able to point to the prerequisite concept they probably missed.

That product idea rests on a layer nobody had shown could be built or trusted: a map of concepts, finer than courses, with dependencies between them. So before designing a product, we ran a time-boxed spike to answer five questions:

  1. Can catalogue prose be turned into concepts aligned to a shared vocabulary, accurately enough to use?
  2. Can dependencies between concepts be inferred without a model inventing them, and scored against something objective?
  3. Can what an institution publishes be kept strictly apart from what the system guesses?
  4. Can a student's plain-English goal be matched to courses by concept coverage?
  5. Can different catalogue platforms feed one common schema?

The spike was built as a research artefact, not a product: one discipline (computer science), one primary institution, loud failures preferred over quiet workarounds.

What the spike answered

Three of the five questions got a clear yes. Dependency inference works but is weak, and the platform question turned up a finding we did not expect.

QuestionAnswerHow strong the evidence is
1. Concepts aligned to a shared vocabulary Yes, with a verifier. 677 concepts from 94 UIUC CS courses, each aligned to one of 81 CS2023 knowledge units or left unaligned. Moderate. An LLM judge put alignment precision at 0.649; a verifier fix then rejected all 9 known false matches and kept all 4 true ones. Not yet re-measured across the full set.
2. Concept dependencies, inferred and scored Partly. Structural signals recover 48% of withheld prerequisites, but precision is 0.073 (F1 0.127). Weak to moderate. The withheld set is drawn after inference ran, so the recall figure is not leakage-free.
3. Published and guessed facts kept apart Yes. They are separate types, enforced when records are built, not by naming convention. Strong. Covered by the 241-test suite.
4. Plain-English goal to courses Yes, end to end. A query resolves to knowledge units, then to courses ranked by relevance and “N of M concepts covered”. Demonstrated. An 18-query relevance evaluation exists; its results are not recorded in the repository.
5. Many platforms, one schema Yes for the schema, no for scraping. Three adapters feed one schema. Purdue's Modern Campus Acalog pages sat behind bot protection, which pushed the spike to UIUC's static catalogue. Strong. The realistic route to Acalog catalogues is the vendor API, not public HTML.

The most reusable outcome may not be the code. It is the evaluation method: several separate numbers instead of one flattering score, failures always reported, and each caveat stated next to the number it qualifies.

Architecture

Granular is four offline stages that write two stores. Everything a student touches only reads those stores.

Two layers, kept apart. The structural record holds what the institution publishes: courses, requirement rules and prerequisites. Each record carries its source URL, retrieval time and adapter version. The concept graph holds what the system infers: concepts, their CS2023 alignment and the dependencies between them.

Alignment only ever narrows. Each extracted concept is matched to one CS2023 knowledge unit in four stages:

  1. Retrieve the top candidates by embedding similarity.
  2. Rerank them with co-occurrence, course level and a small department nudge (+0.05, never a filter).
  3. Reject any candidate that would break a published prerequisite order.
  4. Ask an LLM whether the winner is genuinely that topic, not a word overlap.

No stage can add a candidate an earlier stage removed. If the LLM check fails, the concept keeps its match rather than losing it to an outage.

No model decides a dependency. Edges are scored from structure alone: course level (weight 0.45), published prerequisites (0.40) and knowledge-unit co-occurrence (0.15). An edge never points from a lower-level course to a higher one, and cycles are broken at their weakest edge.

Serving is read-only. A plain-English query is embedded, matched to knowledge units in pgvector, and filtered by an LLM for intent. Courses come back with a relevance score and a coverage count such as “8 of 11 concepts”. A guard blocks wording like “you can take” or “you should”, because the system has no authority to grant anything. No student data is stored: a completed-course list lives for one request only.

All graph queries are plain openCypher, with no Neo4j extensions. Moving to Amazon Neptune or Memgraph should be a configuration change, not a rewrite.

How a course is stored

A course lives in three places. On disk, the canonical record keeps everything parsed from the catalogue, including each prerequisite rule's verbatim wording.

The extraction step loads each course into Neo4j as a declared Course node, linked to its prerequisites by PREREQUISITE edges. Programme records are not loaded; they stay on disk.

Concepts are inferred nodes. Each points back to its course (EXTRACTED_FROM) and to one CS2023 knowledge unit (ALIGNED_TO). DEPENDS_ON links concepts across courses: it is the inferred counterpart of a declared prerequisite, and it records the confidence and model behind it.

Embeddings sit in a separate pgvector table, one row per concept or knowledge unit. They match graph nodes by id only; nothing enforces the link.

How we built it with Kiro

The spike took five days, 8 to 12 September 2026: 57 commits across 21 pull requests. It was spec-first. Requirements and designs for all six packages were in the repository before the first line of code.

01 · The brief

The project brief doubled as steering. It was written to be loaded straight into Kiro. Capabilities were phrased in EARS notation, the rules were written as enforceable invariants, and section numbers were kept stable so a session could be pointed at “§5.2”.

02 · Steering

Three steering files, loaded into every session. All three are marked inclusion: always:

  • product.md sets the scope and lists deliberate cuts, such as no student records.
  • structure.md fixes the stack, the invariants, the spec order and the vocabulary.
  • decisions.md records settled choices and says plainly that “a Kiro spec session must not relitigate them”.
03 · Specs

One spec per package, in dependency order. The order was canonical schema, catalogue ingestion, concept extraction, graph inference, evaluation harness, then advisory queries. Each spec has a requirements.md with numbered EARS criteria and a design.md. Here is part of one requirement:

### REQ-CE-02 — Concept extraction from course descriptions

- FOR EACH Course record with a non-empty description, THE SYSTEM SHALL
  run concept extraction and produce one or more Concept records.
- THE SYSTEM SHALL use a language model for extraction and SHALL record
  the model identifier on every Concept record per REQ-CS-08.
04 · Pull requests

One package per pull request. All six packages landed on day one, with the test suite growing from 34 to 147 tests.

05 · Drift

Specs were corrected when reality disagreed. The ingestion design gained a “Source reality” section once Purdue's pages turned out to be blocked. The evaluation harness's tasks.md was later rewritten to mark each task done, adapted or partly done.

Three lessons stood out:

  • A settled-decisions file saves a lot of time. Without it, each new session tends to reopen scope.
  • Rules enforced in code outlast rules in prose. Because the invariants live in the schema's constructors, generated code could not quietly break them.
  • Specs drift quickly. Day two was mostly about what the specs had not foreseen, so the specs have to be updated as reality lands, not afterwards.

The evaluations also surfaced things that were not bugs, and the spike wrote them down instead of hiding them. The structural reject stage turned out to be redundant: edges can never point upward in course level, so they cannot form the cycles it exists to remove. And the headline recall is not leakage-free, because the withheld prerequisites are chosen after inference has already seen them.

What it did not answer, and what comes next

The biggest open question is grain. The spike only ever read catalogue descriptions of about 100 words per course. Weekly learning objectives are a different kind of text, and nobody has yet tested whether they yield a useful concept graph.

Other gaps:

  • Intervention is untested. No learner data was used, so we do not know whether concept dependencies predict where students actually struggle.
  • Novel edges were never judged. The blinded expert review of inferred edges is implemented but has not been run. Until it runs, the pipeline's contribution beyond published prerequisites is unproven.
  • The numbers are hard to reproduce. They come from commit messages; no evaluation run artefacts are committed to the repository.
  • The prerequisite parser loses choices. For CS 374, “One of CS 173, MATH 213; CS 225” becomes three separate requirements, so the “one of” is lost.
  • Breadth is narrow. It covers one institution and one discipline. The CS2023 vocabulary file is a careful reconstruction, not a parse of the standard.

The next step is a second, narrow spike on grain before any production build:

  1. Take a handful of courses with public week-by-week objectives.
  2. Extract and align concepts at module level.
  3. Infer dependencies between modules, across courses.
  4. Test “struggled in module N, so the missing prerequisite is X” against blinded expert judgement, using the same evaluation method.
The bar for building

If that holds, the production platform has something proven to rest on. If it does not, we find out before building one.
